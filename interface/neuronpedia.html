What is Neural Transparency?

LLMs encode behavioral traits as linear directions in their activation space. By measuring cosine similarity between a model's activation and a pre-extracted behavioral vector, we compute a behavioral score that quantifies trait expression directly from the model's internals.

Reading the Visualization

Red = negative behaviors (Toxic, Hallucinatory, Sycophantic, Antisocial, Discouraging, Unempathetic)

Grey = neutral style (Sophisticated, Casual, Formal, etc.) — bars extend proportionally to activation %.

The Science of Neural Transparency

Mechanistic Interpretability × Human-AI Interaction

The Problem: Behavioral Opacity in LLMs

Large language models are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains — healthcare, education, legal advice, mental health support. Yet their behavior can shift unpredictably across conversation turns. A model might start helpful and honest, then gradually drift toward sycophancy, toxicity, or hallucination as the dialogue evolves. Current chat interfaces give users zero visibility into these internal changes.

This creates a fundamental asymmetry: the model's internal state evolves continuously, but the user's mental model of the AI remains static — anchored to first impressions and surface-level text. The result is miscalibration: users cannot accurately predict, detect, or respond to behavioral drift.

Baseline: How Well Do People Predict AI Behavior?

We conducted a controlled study with N = 246 participants recruited via Prolific, balanced for age, gender, and AI experience. Participants were assigned to one of two system-prompt personas (Default Assistant or Bold Roleplay) and asked to predict the model's behavioral profile across six dimensions.

The results were sobering. Participants achieved a baseline calibration error of ~0.65 RMSE (on a 0–1 scale) with near-chance accuracy (~52%) at identifying whether a trait was positive or negative — barely better than flipping a coin.

More concerning: after a 10-minute conversation, calibration did not improve. Error increased by +0.07 RMSE. Chatting gave participants a false sense of understanding while their actual predictions got worse.

Behavioral Vectors: From Activations to Scores

The core insight from mechanistic interpretability is that LLMs encode high-level behavioral traits as linear directions in their activation space. This means we can extract a "behavioral vector" for any trait (e.g., sycophancy, toxicity, empathy) and measure how strongly a model expresses that trait at any point during generation.

Behavioral Score Computation
score = cos(activation, behavioral_vector)

// score in [-1, 1]
// +1 = maximum trait expression
// -1 = maximum opposite expression
//  0 = neutral / no signal

Results

Finding 1 — Visualization improves calibration: Neural Transparency significantly improved calibration across all metrics compared to control (p < .01, Cohen's d = 0.35–0.49). Both single-turn and multi-turn conditions outperformed control, but multi-turn showed the largest effect.

Finding 2 — Multi-turn outperforms single-turn for drift: When measuring how well participants tracked behavioral changes over the conversation, multi-turn visualization significantly outperformed single-turn (p = .024, d = 0.38). Dynamic, turn-by-turn updates are necessary — a static snapshot is not enough.

Finding 3 — The overconfidence trap: Control participants reported increased predictive confidence after chatting despite showing no actual improvement. Visualization users maintained calibrated confidence — neural transparency tempers the illusion of understanding.

Finding 4 — Greatest benefit for volatile personas: Benefits were strongest for the Bold Roleplay persona (more behaviorally volatile), where multi-turn visualization closed the calibration gap with the more predictable Default Assistant.
